title: Five Kinds of Bananas and a Market That Wastes Nothing
---

I went back to the market this morning. It's just a few blocks behind our hotel. When I think of an ideal place to live, I think of two things: one, near the water for Thomas, and two, walking distance from an open market. This market is exactly that.

I wanted to take a lot more pictures of each stall — how a woman doesn't just sell shallots and onions, she sells many varieties of shallots, onions, and garlic. Which of the five varieties of bananas would you like this morning? The dry noodles — and there are dozens of types — are sold in individual serving sizes, the way medicine capsules and tablets are sold by the blister pack. I think this is so smart, no waste or spoilage. I think of the giant medication bottles I have at home, bought from Costco. Okay, so buying in bulk saves money, but 80% of that bottle goes unused and the label says it expired three years ago.

I ended up not taking many pictures at all because I felt self-conscious about violating their space. They are here to sell; I was supposed to be there to buy. Clearly I wasn't, so the relationship wasn't symbiotic. I was the intruder, snapping some pictures only to benefit me, so I stopped.

I'm no longer alarmed by the slabs of meat sold without ice or refrigeration. Markets like this run on turnover, not cold storage — meat gets sold and cooked the same day it arrives, sometimes within hours, so there's no window for it to sit around going bad the way it would in a fridge nobody opens for a week.

You know the saying that someone swears like a sailor? Here in Vietnam, the phrase is she swears like a woman selling fish at the market. And I witnessed this firsthand the first time I came back to Vietnam. Oh my, the sailors would blush or bow in submission — they are no match.

There are two carts right outside our hotel, one selling fried bananas and fried yams, the other selling gỏi đu đủ bò khô — green papaya salad with beef jerky. The salad was okay, but the fried bananas were exactly as I remembered them from 50 years ago. We bought from her three evenings in a row. She asked if Thomas was my husband. I said yes and volunteered that he's my third. She asked if he had a brother or friend for her. I translated to Thomas and he nodded a big fat lie.

I'm well aware that many Vietnamese women, especially older ones, believe that being married to an American would somehow give them an easier life, or simply that life in America is one of comfort, if not modest luxury. The irony for me is knowing how hard I've worked my entire life. Teaching never ended at 5:00 PM, and certainly not on a Friday. If anything, life in the States is incredibly hectic, especially if you have young children. In Vietnam, two parents working would definitely afford you some housekeeping help, and even a nanny. Now that's luxury! I'm willing to bet that the fried bananas lady thinks Thomas provides for me and I get to go shopping and be pampered at salons. She imagines a world for me that doesn't exist, and I want to warn her that the grass is not always greener elsewhere — but who am I to quash her dream.
